6.3.14 Crew reduction 
Crew reduction is an important issue and many 
discussions have taken place if a two-,man crew can 
be implemented safely and effectively on board omni­
directional harbour tugs. 
Toe question is an economical one, as it plays only 
a role in countries and ports with a sound financial

background. ln many other countries where wages 
are low a relatively high number of crew members are 
welcome; the more people at work the better. 
Because it is economically driven, the discussion about 
two-man crew operations is often difficult, a discussion 
between economics and safety, between theory and 
pradice, between two total different professions. 
Nevertheless, under certain conditions two-man crew 
operations are allowed in some countries and ports, such 
as in the Port of Hamburg, the Port of Rotterdam, in 
Canadian and some New Zealand ports. 
The Hamburg Sta te government permits two-man crew 
operations within the port limits. A qualified captain 
and engineer are then needed. The wheelhouse has to 
comply with certain requirements, such as with respect 
to visibility from the wheelhouse, handling of controls, 
radar, wheelhouse windows and VHF radio equipment, 
of which one should be equipped with foot control, 
engine controls, indicators and alarms. The tug should be 
equipped with two independent working main engines. 
Towing winch and towing hook should meet certain 
requirements, such as with regard to operating the 
towing winch and the availability of quick release 
systems for the towing winch as well as for the towing 
hook. 
Life-saving equipment should be located such that it 
can be put into the water by one crewmember. There 
should be a doar in the bulwarks for use in case of a man 
overboard. Deck floodlighting should be installed. 
Port of Rotterdam has a number of similar requirements. 
Also a qualified crew of a captain and engineer is 
needed. Further requirements among others are the 
ability to manoeuvre the tug during ship assistance 
with one engine only, deck loudhailer, towing winch 
to be controlled on deck by a portable control unit, 
an aluminium ladder, life net at both sides of the tug. 
Crewmember on deck should wear certified safety 
helmet, safety shoes and Personal Floatation Device (eg 
self-inflating life jacket). Important for the Rotterdam's 
requirement is that a third crew member should be on 
board in the case of visibility being less than 300m, ice 
on deck, winds of 7 Beaufort or more, sea state 3 -4 and 
when towing operations are carried out outside the port 
at a maximum distance of 5 nautical miles from the pier 
heads. 
Canadian regulations are such that tugs below '300 GT 
and below 20m are allowed to operate with a crew of 
two. Tugs of at least 300 GT but less than 500 GT that 
want to operate with a two-man crew have to meet a 
number of requirements partly similar to those in the 
Port of Hamburg or Rotterdam. ln addition: The tug is 
being used to assist a vessel to dock or undock and is at 
no time more than five nautical miles from an accessible 
dock offering refuge, meets the standards for periodically 
unattended machinery spaces; a shore engineer should be 
available, etc.

The manoeuvres to be carried out by the tug should also

Day boats. Will the tugs operate 24 hours a day or is it

Releasing tugs 
Releasing tugs should take place when it can be expected

the risk of groundings in such a situation, as is the case

Different types of hook in addition to the radial towing

hook and deck construction.

length. A towing winch allows faster and easier handling 
of the towline, especially when heavy lines are used. 
Automatic spooling gears, which spool the towline 
properly around the towing drum, may sometimes be 
found on harbour towing winches. However, because 
of the short towlines used in harbour towage in relation 
to the long towlines at sea, many harbour towing 
winches are not equipped with a spooling gear. When so 
equipped they are not always used, particularly when 
a steel wire towline with stretcher and pendant is used. 
The spooling gear is then of little use for the short part of 
the main towline. 
Towing winches are driven through reduction gears by 
a motor which may be powered by hydraulic pressure 
or electricity. There are many variations in electric and 
hydraulic drive types that may significântly influence 
performance, reliability and costs. One example is the 
frequency-controlled towing winch. Compared to a 
hydraulically driven winch, a frequency-controlled 
winch has a number of advantages, such as a smooth 
and stepless control, easier installation, space savings 
(no hydraulic power package required) and imm.ediate 
readiness for use. The control system, if needed, can 
be arranged under deck, so avoiding corrosion and 
moisture. Main advantage of electric driven winch is 
drive efficiency. Hydraulic systems typically lose 20-
40 per cent of power (low /high pressure) and electric 
winches typically 10-15 per cent (approximately half by 
motor and half by drive). This is of special interest in case 
of continuous use; if only used occasionally as in many 
harbour tugs, efficiency is of less importance. ln recent 
years, mass applications throughoutthe industry have 
resulted in a large cost reduction of frequency drives.

For ship assistance by harbour tugs, the following aspects 
of a towing winch are important:

•

Brake holding capacity is the holding capacity of the brake 
and refers to the firstlayer on the drum. Toe more layers, 
the lower the braking capacity. Brakmg capacity of towing 
winches for harbour towing is two to three times the 
bollard pull of the tug, although it is often dependent on a 
towing company's policy. Lower values can be found such 
as a brake holding capacity equal to bollard pull. 
There is an important relationship between brake 
holding capacity and the minimum breaking strength of 
174 Tug Use in Port 
a towline. With a low brake holding capacity and a high 
strength towline (towline with high safety factor), the 
brake may slip before the towline breaks (see note below 
about winch brake), preventing too high loads in the 
towline and consequently lengthening the towline's life. 
However, a relatively low brake holding capacity may 
limit a tug's performance because in certain situations, 
such as steep towline angles, towline forces can be much 
higher than bollard pull and in order to avoid brake 
slippage tug power then has to be reduced. 
The opposite can also be the case. With a high brake 
holding capacity, eg three times the bollard pull, the 
towline may break before the brake slips, unless the 
quiék release is used in time. On the other hand a tug's 
performance is less limited, which can be of importance 
during ship assistance in adverse weather and/ or current 
conditions. Therefore when building a new tug, the brake 
holding capacity of the towing winch is an important 
factor to be carefully considered in relation to the 
minimum breaking strength of the towline and required 
performance of the tug. 
It should be noted that with a steadily increasing towline 
force the brake may slip at the brake holding power of 
the winch. However, in case of shock loads the brake 
will mostly not slip at that stage due to the inertia of 
the braking system and the .towline may break. Modem 
towing winches have an adjustable brake holding power, 
with automatic release on a preset line tension, while 
winch brakes open in case of 'dead ship'. They even may 
have a water-cooled variable braking feature (WCVS) as 
a back-up system for in case a problem develops with the 
band brakes. Toe WCVS brake is a so called load neutral 
brake, whereby the release load (torque) is independent 
of the towline load applied (in many cases tnultiple plate 
clutches). To.is brake offers fine control for a tension slip­
point, which is not possible with band brakes (not load 
neutral). These aspects will be given further attention 
when discussing the various winches according the way 
forces in the towline can be controlled. 
One important factor to be aware of is the elasticity of 
the towline. The load in a towline with a stretcher may 
increase with eg 50 ton per second; a full Dyneema line 
may increase with values five times as high. Time by the 
winch to react is determined by the elasticity; with fu11 
Dyneema towlines the brake system should instantly 
react within less than one second. 
Other important aspects are the maximum pulling 
capacity and pull/speed characteristics of a towing winch. 
Toe maximum pulling capacity of a winch is the stall

diameter of the first layer, hence the pull and hold will 
be only 50 per cent. Example: If the brake holding power 
of the winch is 100 tons and the tug is working with a 
towline coming from the top layer of the drum, the winch 
will slip with a towline force of approximately 50 tons 
instead of 100 tons.

Figure 7.28 shows friction coefficients of the various fibre

•

rape temperature reaches its operating limit. Care

Friction causes abrasion. The greater the friction the 
greater the abrasion. This effect assumes a sliding

a fairlead. The rougher the surface of the fairlead, the
greater the friction and, consequently, the abrasion.

Small dianGeter fairleads A very important point. Bending

load within separate components of the rope. This will 
significantly increase the risks associated with inter-fibre 
abrasion, heat generation and flex fatigue. Long-lay 
constructions, with low or zero helix angles and tightly 
bound jackets, are particularly prone to damage (MAIB 
ReportlNG carrier Zarga).

prepared and maintained surfaces.

SWL ship's towing equipment 
The bollard pull of harbour tugs has increased 
considerably from about 20 -30 tons up to around 70 
- 80 tons nowadays, with up to 100 tons and more for
terminal tugs, in a period of approximately 30 - 40 years.
These powerful terminal tugs often operate in port areas
as well.
This has three important consequences for ship handling 
with tugs: 
•
The strength of bitts, fairleads and supporting
structures of the ships being assisted did not
keep pace with the increasing power of tugs and
consequently increasing tow line forces. This has
resulted in situations where bollards and/ or fairleads
have been damaged or even rupturedfrom their
foundations. There is no clear relation between the
power of tugs and the strength of ship's bitts and
fairleads.
•
Another consequence is that tugs have to reduce
power in such a way that bitts and fairleads can
cope with the towline force. ln other words, the tugs
should pull with such an amount of power that the
towline force on ship's bitts and fairleads is not more
than their respective SWL values.
•
Pulling with reduced power has the consequence
of increased risk for the ship to be handled. When
for instance an incoming ship needs two tugs of 70
tons to compensate for the wind pressure, if they
cannot deliver that power the ship may start drifting,
or an extra tug has to be ordered; an unacceptable
situation.

8.1 
Reasons for training 
Training is necessary to become a tug master or pilot. 
In several ports tug master training is still only carried 
out 'on the job' and the same is true for most ports with 
respect to pilot training on the subject of tugs and tug use. 
Training on the job, gathering experience in practice, is 
indeed an essential part of the training to becoming a 
skilled pilot or tug master. There are, however, a number 
of drawbacks. If only using on the job training, a system 
of trial and errar, risks are involved because of the errors. 
It is furthermore time consuming and can therefore be 
expensive, also because of the possible errors. Besides, 
tug masters or pilots only pass on to a trainee the 
experience they have built up themselves. This includes 
any shortcomings and accumulated bad habits and may 
not, therefore, result in the most efficient and safest use 
of tugs. This may particularly be the case when on the job 
training is carried out by just one person. lt means that 
a training system is needed which does not have these 
drawbacks. 
Besides, the need and importance of proper training has 
grown considerably since the appearance of tugs with 
different propulsion systems such as azimuth thrusters or 
Voith Schneider propulsion, and the increased nu.mber of 
tug types, complexity and power of tugs. 
Another reason for a high quality training system is 
that in particular, modem tugs are a costly investment 
and should be used in the most efficient and safe way. 
Pilots and tug masters should therefore possess the 
ability to use or handle a tug to its fullest capabilities. 
Furthermore, port developments don't always keep pace 
,vith increased ship size or draft and a minimum of tugs 
is often used due to economic pressure. This results in 
diminishing safety and operationalmargins and a more 
essential role for the remaining tugs. When tug power 
increases it often results in the use of even fewer tugs per 
ship. This increases the importance, responsibility and 
workload of each individual tug master. 
Another aspect to be aware of is that experience, based 
on proper training, can be gained more quickly, and the 
highest level achieved, when a tug master handles just 
one tug or tug type. When, as is the case in some ports, 
tug masters shift between different tug types, the need 
for proper basic training further increases. 
Ali arguments why a professional high levei training 
is needed, based on the needs of present tug and ship 
handling. Subjects other than tug handling also require 
attention during training. 
Unfortunately a lack of proper training is too often found 
as can be concluded from following observations: 
Ninety per cent of the participants of the 2017 Asian Tug 
Technology & Salvage conference felt that lack of crew 
competence was the most important factor contributing 
to tug accidents and that a mix of enhanced training and 
better operational guidance were the keys to enhance 
safety. 
A report (2016) of a large South American country comes 
with the amazing conclusion that in that specific country 
20 times more tugs sunk in a period of 20 years compared 
to two large European towing companies. Reasons cited 
are a lack of tug master training and ship speeds being 
too high, which is also caused by a lack of training. This 
is a rather dramatic and unacceptable situation. 
Accident investigation reports show the sarne need for 
proper training. See for instance the 2012 accident report 
of the ASD-tug Skuld where the tug master handled the 
thrusters as if the tug was towing over the bow while it 
was towing over the stern. lt resulted in a collision with 
the tow. 
Another example is the capsizing and sinking of the 
twin screw North Tug. The tug assisted the cruise vesséí 
Ocean Princess on departure in June 2013. The tug crew 
had no experience of assisting vessels as large as Ocean 
Princess. The Accident Investigation Board Norway 
concluded, among other things, that " ... the pilot of 
the Ocean Princess did not have sufficient overview 
of the limitations of the North Tug and its crew." (See 
References.) 
From the above it becomes patently clear how important 
a proper and professional training is because it is a 
necessity to be able to carry out towing operations in a 
safe and efficient way. 
There is another aspect to keep in mind. Ever less time 
is made available for training, in particular for training 
on the job. This increases the need for effective training 
programmes, but also to make training on the job much 
more effective. lt includes also an increasing important 
role for training instructors.
