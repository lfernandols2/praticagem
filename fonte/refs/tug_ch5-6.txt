4.3.3 Summary 
Many differences in performance, capabilities and 
limitations of different tug types have been reviewed. 
For the reader's convenience a brief summary follows 
of the most important aspects. It is assumed that ali 
tugs discussed are suitable for their tasks and have the 
required stability, sufficient freeboard, proper towing 
equipment and manoeuvrability. 
Conventional tugs can be very effective when towing on 
a line at a ship having speed through the water. They can

forces, but often also detiver an unwanted force which 
increases a ship' s speed. 
As forward tug on a line a conventional tug can assist 
in steering to both sides but as stem tug it has its 
limitations. At higher speeds, steering âssistance can only 
be given to one side. Qn.ly at very low speeds is steering 
control to both sides and control of ship' s speed possible. 
As both a forward and a stem tug, capsizing (girting) is 
possible as a result of the position of the towing point 
in combination with induced strong transverse forces. 
To minimise risk of girting a completely reliable quick 
release system should be used. A radial towing hook or 
equivalent system also decreases the risk of capsizing. 
The ability to provide stopping assistance is nil for 
forward tugs towing on a line and limited to very low 
speeds for stern tugs towing on a line. Ship's engines 
should be handled with care when conventional tugs are 
dose to the stem. Dueto these limitations as a stem tug, 
tug positions should be carefully planned in advance. 
The pushing effectiveness of conventional tugs decreases 
quickly with increasing ship's speed; pulling is only 
possible at zero or low speeds, depending on whether 
a stem line is used. Ship's speed should be carefully 
conttolled so as to take account of the limited capabilities 
of a conventional tug operating at a ship' s side.

4.3.4 Effectiveness of tug types 
Model testing and full scale trials have been used to 
determine tug capabilities. Most tests focus on the 
abilities of one specific tug or tug type. Voith has done, 
and still does, considerable work regarding VS tractor 
tugs. In the past, Aquamaster carried out several studies 
regarding tugs with azimuth propellers. Recent studies 
and full scale trials that have been undertaken mainly 
assess specific tugs and tug type escorting capabilities. 
Most of these studies and trials, therefore, only involve 
some specific aspects of ship assisting manoeuvres 
required during daily tug handling. Of course, severa! 
variations in the design of a specific type of tug exist. 
Simulation programs provide the possibility of gaining 
insight into a more extensive range of a tug's ability 
using, for example, afull mission bridge simulator. When 
these programs are carried out in close co-operation 
with pilots and tug captains and are, as far as possible, 
verified in full scale trials, the results give quite high 
reliability. Simulations are mainly carried out for one 
specific tug or for a very limited number of tugs of which 
ali details of rudder, propulsion, stability, maximum list, 
hydrodynamic coefficients and so on are known. 
Desktop computer simulation programs exist, based on 
a steady situation -equilibrium of forces - by which 
the performance of different tugs and tug types can be 
determined. With these simulation programs capabilities 
produced by different variations of tug design can be 
predicted, 
Simulatión programs may not normaliy take into account 
accurately ali factors in.fluencing tug performance, such 
as ship-tug interaction, flow field around a ship, influence 
of water depth and confinement on the flow field, and the 
influence of ship's wake on a tug's braking performance, 
which are discussed in Chapters 6. There may, therefore, 
be some inaccuracy in simulation results, depending on 
the situation. Limitations in simulation programs and 
inaccuracies should be known, including the effect on the 
simulation results. 
Real capabilities and in particular limitations are, of 
course, experienced during daily ship handling only, 
but the results of simulation programs can verify what is 
explained in this book.

5.2.1 
The two main situations to be considered 
To determine the total required bollard pull it is 
important to understand the two basic situations: 
•
External forces, such as forces of wind, current and
waves that have to be compensated by tugs, which is
in particular the case with ships with a high windage,
and the
•
Mass of a ship, as is the case with loaded tankers and
bulk carriers.
These are two totally different situations and require a 
separate approach which will be explained first. 
Wind, current and waves create forces on a ship. These 
forces have to be compensated for by forces generated 
by tugs. The forces working on a ship can be calculated 
and based on the results the required tug forces can be 
determined. 
ln case of a loaded tanker or bulk carrier, it is primarily 
the mass of the vessel that counts. How much tug 
power is needed to handle a large loaded tanker? ln 
general currents don't play a role, unless there is some 
cross current near the jetty. Wind has relatively a small 
effect on the tanker. So,how to determine the required 
bollard pull? The required bollard pull is just a matter 
of acceleration and deceleration or in other words how 
fast can tugs stop a sideways movement, give the ship 
a sideways movement or tum a vessel? Any tug force 
would be sufficient if time does not count, beca use F 
= m x a (F = the force; m = the mass of the vessel; a ;::: 
the accelération). The formula shows that in cases of no 
wind, current or waves, even a little force will start to 
move a large vessel. 
However, time counts. Therefore a fast acceleration or 
deceleration is needed and consequently the higher the 
tug force the better. Toe required tug force is therefore 
mainly based on experience. 
ln the following paragraphs, first wind, ctirrent and wave

As mentioned earlier, tugs should have sufficient reserve 
power to stop a drifting ship. A comparable situation 
exists during berthing. An arriving, a ship is stopped 
parallel to a berth or jetty and is then pushed, pulled or 
heaved alongside. Wind, current and even waves may 
also push a ship towards a berth. Due to these forces 
a ship gains transverse speed which should be slowed 
down by tugs to 'dead in the water' or to a safe berthing 
speed at the moment a ship touches the fenders. So, 
tugs have to oppose the forces of wind, current and 
waves, and in addition have to reduce the transverse 
approach speed of a ship towards a berth, which requires 
additional bollard pull. Of course, the wind may blow

or waves, bollard pull is needed to control a ship's 
transverse speed.

stun of displacement and added mass. Toe exact amount 
of added mass is difficult to determine. The added 
mass increases with decreasing underkeel clearance.

and is very large with a sideways motion. It then 
normally varies between 25 per cent to 100 per cent of a 
ship's displacement. Many formulae used for calculating

strong interaction forces, including turning moments, 
around the bow of a ship making way. These interaction

free again. The only safe escape manoeuvre for the tug is 
always Full Astem.

for the interaction forces, because when setting the 
propulsion units away from the bow the tug will move 
away. Conventional tugs, or ASD-tugs operating as a 
conventional tug, when steering away from the bow, will 
experience a force towards the ship and the tug' s stem 
will move quickly inwards. This is a consequence of the 
aft lying rudder(s) and propulsion.

for bow tugs. Problems can arise with ships having a 
very high Dead Slow Ahead speed in combination with 
steering problems when the engine is stopped at a Dead 
Slow speed.
